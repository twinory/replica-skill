The honest limits

No browser can promise zero ads everywhere. These are the cases where the shield stops, so you can decide whether that is good enough before you install it.

1. Ads inside the video stream Two ways an ad reaches you. Separate request: blockable, and Veil blocks it. Stitched into the same stream as the show: not blockable by any browser, because the bytes are the show. YouTube does this for most pre-rolls, Twitch does it, live sport does it. Veil's player patch fast-forwards and skips the skip button where the site still exposes it, and says so when it cannot.
2. DRM protected music and video on iPhone Spotify, Netflix and Prime stream through Widevine. Apple gives apps FairPlay only, and those services do not serve FairPlay to a web view. The result is a spinner or a black screen, not an ad. Veil detects the site, explains it, and offers the real app instead. On Android, Widevine is present in the WebView on certified devices, so it usually plays.
3. Google sign-in Google refuses to render its login page inside any embedded web view, on purpose, since 2017. Spoofing the user agent to get around it breaks Google's terms and gets accounts flagged. Veil sends only that page to Safari or Chrome and the cookie comes back to the tab, so you end up signed in without the app pretending to be something it is not.
4. First party promoted posts A promoted post delivered from the same endpoint as normal posts, with no wrapper element and no label class, is structurally identical to a normal post. Rules cannot tell them apart without breaking the feed. Veil removes the labelled and wrapped ones, which is most of them on most sites.
5. Sites that refuse web views A site can check the user agent, see a web view, and show "open in the app" instead of content. Veil hides those banners, but it cannot make a site believe it is Safari. Some banks and streaming services do this deliberately.
6. Lists rot Ad networks rotate domains weekly. The bundled list is a starting point with a few hundred rules, not a maintained service. Point the update URL at your own fork, or the blocking quality will drift down over months. This is the least glamorous and most important part of running a browser.
7. Store rules Apple rejects thin wrappers and apps whose pitch is "ad-free YouTube". Google Play treats ad blocking as sensitive. The safe route is a real browser with its own features, distributed to testers first, with a name that is not someone else's trademark. That is a business problem, not a code problem.
